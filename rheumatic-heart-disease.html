Rheumatic heart disease

Why this disease is important

When Nigerian schoolchildren are screened with echocardiography, many have rheumatic heart disease that nobody had heard with a stethoscope.

In lower-income countries, rheumatic heart disease is still the leading acquired heart disease of children and young adults.

Rheumatic heart disease starts with a sore throat in childhood and shows itself as a scarred mitral valve years later.

Consider this case

A 23-year-old woman, a tailor, is brought to the emergency unit with weakness of her right arm and the right side of her face that began this morning. For a year she's been breathless on exertion, and for two months she hasn't been able to lie flat.

At 12 she had painful, swollen knees and then ankles, one joint after another, a few weeks after a sore throat. She was treated at a chemist's shop and never had injections afterwards.

On examination her pulse is irregularly irregular, and there's a rumbling mid-diastolic murmur at the apex. A CT scan of the head shows an infarct in the left cerebral hemisphere. Echocardiography shows thickened mitral leaflets with fused commissures, a dilated left atrium and a thrombus in it. She starts warfarin, furosemide, digoxin and benzathine penicillin injections.

Definition of rheumatic heart disease

Rheumatic heart disease is defined by what acute rheumatic fever does to the heart, during the attack and after it heals.

Acute rheumatic fever is an acute, immune-mediated inflammatory disease of several organs that follows a group A streptococcal infection, usually of the throat. Rheumatic heart disease is its cardiac form: a with and in the acute attack, and scarred, deformed valves with in the chronic phase.

Gross morphology of rheumatic heart disease

Key facts

  • Acute rheumatic valvulitis leaves small warty verrucae in a row along the lines of closure.
  • Fibrous bridging across fused commissures makes a fish-mouth or buttonhole mitral stenosis.
  • Rheumatic heart disease scars the mitral valve alone in most cases, and the pulmonary valve almost never.

Rheumatic heart disease changes the valve in two stages. The acute attack leaves small vegetations, and later scarring narrows the valve for good.

Valves scarred by rheumatic heart disease

  • Mitral valve alone: most patients.
  • Mitral and aortic valves together: most of the rest.
  • Tricuspid valve: less often, and less severely.
  • Pulmonary valve: almost never.

Aetiology of acute rheumatic fever

A group A streptococcus causes acute rheumatic fever, but only in a few of the people it infects. Age, living conditions and genes raise the risk.

Quick checkWhy doesn't every child with a streptococcal sore throat get rheumatic fever?

The strain and the host. Antibodies and T cells cross-react with the heart only after certain streptococcal strains, and only one to three in every hundred infected people get rheumatic fever, so genetic susceptibility is likely.

Key facts

Jones criteria for acute rheumatic fever

Key facts

  • Acute rheumatic fever needs recent streptococcal infection plus two major, or one major and two minor, Jones criteria.
  • Rheumatic polyarthritis moves from one large joint to the next and heals with no lasting damage.
  • Carditis is the main feature of acute rheumatic fever in children; arthritis dominates in adults.

The Jones criteria diagnose acute rheumatic fever from its features in the heart, joints, skin and nervous system.

Connect this to other specialties

Acute rheumatic fever is diagnosed from evidence of a recent streptococcal infection, usually raised antibodies to streptococcal antigens, plus two major criteria, or one major and two minor criteria. Course of rheumatic heart disease

Key facts

  • Very few patients die of the acute attack of rheumatic fever.
  • Rheumatic carditis worsens with each recurrence, and the damage is cumulative.
  • Chronic rheumatic heart disease usually shows itself years or decades after the first attack.

Rheumatic heart disease starts with an attack in childhood and shows itself as valve disease years later. The valve narrows slowly, and the left atrium, lungs and right heart change with it.

Connect this to other specialties

Course of rheumatic heart disease, from first attack to valve disease

  1. Acute rheumatic fever is commonest at five to fifteen years of age, and carditis is its main feature. Very few patients die of the acute attack.

  2. The first attack leaves immune memory of the streptococcus. Each later streptococcal infection can set off a new attack of rheumatic fever.

  3. Carditis worsens with each recurrence, and the damage adds up. Each attack heals by more scarring of the valve.

  4. Chronic rheumatic heart disease usually isn't clinically evident until years or decades after the first attack. A narrowing mitral valve is well tolerated until late.

  5. Murmurs, an enlarged heart and heart failure appear, often with atrial fibrillation and emboli from clots in the atrium. The signs depend on which valves are scarred and how badly.

Complications of rheumatic heart disease

Key facts

  • A dilated, fibrillating left atrium causes stasis, and large mural thrombi form in it.
  • Emboli from a left atrial thrombus lodge in end arteries of the brain, kidneys or spleen and cause infarcts.
  • In pure mitral stenosis the right ventricle fails and the left ventricle is usually normal.

Most complications of rheumatic heart disease start in the left atrium behind the narrow mitral valve. Clots form there and embolise to the brain, and back pressure falls on the lungs and the right heart.

Complications of mitral stenosis, step by step

Step 1 of 6

A narrow valve

Scarred, fused leaflets narrow the mitral opening, so blood can't leave the left atrium easily. Pressure in the left atrium rises, and the atrium dilates from the pressure load.

The atrium fibrillates

The dilated left atrium starts to fibrillate. Atrial fibrillation causes turbulent flow, and together with the dilated atrium it causes stasis.

Mural thrombus

Stasis in the dilated, fibrillating atrium lets large form against its wall.

Embolus to the brain

Pieces of the thrombus break off into the systemic arteries. The brain, kidneys and spleen are frequent targets, and an embolus that blocks an end artery causes an . In the brain, that infarct is a stroke.

Infective endocarditis

Key facts

  • Infective endocarditis forms friable, bulky vegetations of fibrin, inflammatory cells and organisms.
  • Infective endocarditis affects the aortic and mitral valves most; the tricuspid in people who inject drugs.
  • Infected emboli from vegetations cause septic infarcts and mycotic aneurysms where they lodge.

Infective endocarditis can settle on a normal valve, but a valve scarred by rheumatic heart disease is more easily infected. Its vegetations destroy the valve and shed infected emboli.

Group A streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes)

Key facts

  • Group A streptococcus is β-haemolytic on blood agar, but viridans streptococci are α-haemolytic.
  • M protein, a major virulence factor of group A streptococcus, blocks complement activation and protects it from phagocytosis.
  • Streptolysin O is antigenic, so infection is followed by anti-streptolysin O antibodies, the ASO titre.

Group A streptococcus causes the sore throat that starts rheumatic fever. Its wall antigens and toxins explain both the disease and the tests for it.

Connect this to other specialties

Group A streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes) is a Gram-positive coccus that grows in chains, is catalase negative and causes on blood agar. It belongs to A because of the C carbohydrate in its cell wall.

Group A streptococcus carries M protein and makes toxins and enzymes. Only some of them leave an antibody that a blood test can measure.

Streptococcal pharyngitis and rheumatic fever

Key facts

  • Acute rheumatic fever starts two to three weeks after a group A streptococcal pharyngitis.
  • By the time rheumatic fever starts, streptococci are absent from the lesions and throat cultures are usually negative.

Rheumatic fever is a late result of a streptococcal sore throat. By the time it starts, the infection is over.

Connect this to other specialties

Rheumatic fever after streptococcal pharyngitis, step by step

  1. Group A streptococcus infects the throat, causing red, swollen tonsils with pus on them, high fever and swollen cervical lymph nodes. The sore throat settles within a few days. Viruses can cause the same picture, so the throat alone doesn't prove the cause.

  2. B cells make antibodies against and other streptococcal antigens, and T cells respond to M protein too. The response takes weeks to build.

  3. Antibodies against M protein the streptococci, and macrophages and neutrophils destroy them.

  4. Two to three weeks after the sore throat, fever and migratory polyarthritis start, often with carditis. Antibodies and T cells against M protein now recognise proteins in the heart. Streptococci are gone from the lesions, and throat cultures are usually negative.

Remember. Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis is the other delayed disease of group A streptococcus. It follows a throat or skin infection, and immune complexes deposited in the glomeruli cause the injury.

Prothrombin time and INR

Key facts

  • Warfarin lowers the vitamin K-dependent factors II, VII, IX and X, and the fall in VII, X and II lengthens the prothrombin time.
  • The INR corrects the prothrombin time for the thromboplastin reagent, so results agree between laboratories.
  • The target INR on warfarin in atrial fibrillation is 2.0 to 3.0.

The prothrombin time, reported as the INR, measures how far warfarin has lowered the vitamin K-dependent clotting factors.

Connect this to other specialties

How the prothrombin time and INR are measured

  1. Blood for clotting tests goes into a sodium citrate tube. Citrate binds calcium, so the blood can't clot in the tube, and adding calcium back reverses it. EDTA also binds calcium, but calcium can't reverse it, so an EDTA sample is unsuitable.

  2. Tissue factor and phospholipid, together called thromboplastin, are added to the plasma with calcium. Thromboplastin starts clotting through factor VII, the extrinsic pathway, and on through factors X, V, II and fibrinogen, the common pathway.

  3. The is the seconds the plasma takes to clot. Warfarin lowers factors VII, X and II, so the prothrombin time rises.

  4. Thromboplastin reagents differ in sensitivity between laboratories. The divides the patient's prothrombin time by the laboratory's normal mean and corrects for the reagent's sensitivity, so one sample gives the same INR anywhere.

Quick checkWarfarin also lowers factor IX. Why doesn't the prothrombin time pick that up?

Factor IX belongs to the intrinsic pathway, and the prothrombin time tests only the extrinsic pathway (factor VII) and the common pathway (X, V, II and fibrinogen). The APTT tests the intrinsic pathway, factor IX among it.

Benzathine penicillin and secondary prophylaxis of rheumatic fever

Key facts

  • Penicillin binds penicillin-binding proteins and blocks transpeptidation, so a growing bacterium can't build its wall.
  • Benzathine penicillin is absorbed slowly from muscle, so one injection gives low but long-lasting penicillin levels.
  • Each new attack of rheumatic fever adds to the valve damage, so benzathine penicillin continues for years, often for life.

Benzathine penicillin is a slow-release form of penicillin G, injected into muscle. Repeated injections stop new streptococcal infections, so a patient who has had rheumatic fever doesn't have another attack.

Connect this to other specialties

Mechanism of benzathine penicillin in secondary prophylaxis of rheumatic fever

  1. Benzathine penicillin is a salt of penicillin G made to be absorbed slowly. Injected into muscle, it releases penicillin over a long time, so blood and tissue levels stay low but last.

  2. Penicillin binds and blocks transpeptidation, the cross-linking of peptidoglycan. A growing group A streptococcus can't build its wall, and it dies.

  3. Repeated injections keep penicillin in the tissues, so a group A streptococcus reaching the throat is killed before it can cause a new infection.

  4. After a first attack, each new streptococcal infection can set off rheumatic fever again. Carditis gets worse with each recurrence and the damage adds up, so each attack prevented spares the valve.

  5. In Nigeria benzathine penicillin is continued for years after an attack of rheumatic fever, often for life.

Aspirin in acute rheumatic fever

Key facts

  • Aspirin blocks cyclooxygenase, so fewer prostaglandins are made and inflammation and fever fall.
  • Aspirin irritates the stomach and can cause gastric and duodenal ulcers; like other NSAIDs it can cause tinnitus.
  • Aspirin isn't given to children or adolescents with a viral infection, because of the risk of Reye syndrome.

In Nigeria aspirin is used for the arthritis and fever of acute rheumatic fever. Aspirin's benefit and much of its harm come from blocking cyclooxygenase.

Mechanism of aspirin

  1. Aspirin blocks (COX). The body breaks aspirin down to salicylate within minutes, and salicylate carries most of the anti-inflammatory effect.

  2. Cyclooxygenase makes prostaglandins from arachidonic acid. With fewer prostaglandins, inflammation, pain and fever fall, so the swollen joints and fever of acute rheumatic fever settle.

  3. Aspirin irreversibly acetylates COX-1 in platelets. Platelets can't make new enzyme, so they make less thromboxane for the rest of their life. Other cells make new enzyme within hours.

Each finding points to one harm of aspirin.

Warfarin: mechanism, adverse effects and drug interactions

Key facts

  • Warfarin blocks vitamin K epoxide reductase, so factors II, VII, IX and X are made without their γ-carboxyl groups.
  • Metronidazole, co-trimoxazole, fluconazole and erythromycin raise the INR; rifampicin lowers it.
  • Warfarin crosses the placenta, and early in pregnancy it causes fetal warfarin syndrome, with abnormal bone formation.

Warfarin prevents a thrombus forming in the dilated, fibrillating left atrium. Warfarin's effect varies from patient to patient and many drugs raise or lower it, so it's followed with the INR.

Connect this to other specialties

Mechanism of warfarin

  1. Active vitamin K is the cofactor for γ-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX and X and of proteins C and S. Each carboxylation turns the vitamin into its inactive epoxide.

  2. turns the epoxide back into active vitamin K. Warfarin blocks this enzyme, so active vitamin K runs out.

  3. The liver goes on making factors II, VII, IX and X, but without γ-carboxyglutamate residues they can't take part in clotting. The prothrombin time and the INR rise.

  4. Factors already in the blood keep working until they're broken down. Factor VII falls first and prothrombin last, so warfarin takes days to reach its full effect.

  5. Protein C, a natural anticoagulant, falls as fast as factor VII, so for a short time early in treatment the blood clots more easily. In a patient with inherited protein C deficiency, skin necrosis can follow in the first weeks.

Pot station
A heart from a 30-year-old woman, opened from above, shows the mitral valve seen from the left atrium. The opening is a narrow slit, the leaflets are thick, and the left atrium is large. Describe the specimen and name the lesion.

A heart opened from above, with a narrowed, thickened mitral valve and a dilated left atrium. The lesion is mitral stenosis, and rheumatic heart disease is almost its only acquired cause.

What would you look for on the leaflets and chordae to show the stenosis is rheumatic?

Fused commissures, with fibrous bridging across them, and short, thick, fused chordae tendineae. Fused commissures point to past inflammation; calcific degeneration doesn't fuse them.

Why is the left atrium so large?

Blood can't leave the left atrium easily through the narrow valve, so the pressure in it rises and the atrium dilates from the pressure load.

What might you find inside that atrium, and what can it cause?

A mural thrombus. The dilated, fibrillating atrium causes stasis, and pieces of the thrombus can embolise to the brain, kidneys or spleen and cause infarcts.

Which chamber would you expect to be normal, and why?

The left ventricle. In pure mitral stenosis the block sits above it, so the pressure builds in the left atrium, the lungs and the right ventricle.

Pot station
A heart valve from a 35-year-old man carries a bulky, irregular, friable mass on one leaflet, and part of the cusp beneath it is destroyed. Name the lesion.

A large, friable, destructive vegetation of infective endocarditis.

What is the vegetation made of?

Fibrin, inflammatory cells and organisms, built up on the valve as thrombotic debris.

How would rheumatic verrucae look different?

Rheumatic verrucae are small and warty, in a row along the lines of closure. They don't destroy the valve, and they hold no organisms.

The vegetation has eroded into the myocardium below the valve. What has it formed?

A ring abscess: an abscess cavity in the myocardium around the valve ring.

Pieces break off. What do they cause where they lodge?

Septic infarcts, which turn into abscesses because the embolus holds organisms, and mycotic aneurysms where infection weakens an artery wall.

Slide station
A section of myocardium from a 10-year-old girl who died in an attack of acute rheumatic fever shows an interstitial focus of lymphocytes, plasma cells and plump macrophages around pink, smudgy necrosis. Name the lesion.

An Aschoff body, the pathognomonic lesion of acute rheumatic fever.

What kind of necrosis sits at its centre?

Fibrinoid necrosis.

In which layers of the heart can Aschoff bodies form?

All three: the pericardium, the myocardium and the endocardium, so acute rheumatic carditis is a pancarditis.

What would the same heart show on the mitral valve?

Fibrinoid necrosis and fibrin along the lines of closure, forming small, warty verrucae.

Why are Aschoff bodies rarely seen in a chronic rheumatic valve?

As the acute inflammation heals, fibrous scar with new vessels replaces them.

Slide station
At high power, an inflammatory focus in the myocardium of a child with acute rheumatic fever holds plump cells with abundant cytoplasm. Their chromatin is condensed into a slender, wavy central ribbon. Name these cells.

Anitschkow cells, also called caterpillar cells for their wavy ribbon of chromatin.

What kind of cell is an Anitschkow cell?

An activated macrophage of the Aschoff body.

How do you tell them from Langhans giant cells?

Each Anitschkow cell has one nucleus with a wavy ribbon of chromatin. Langhans giant cells are multinucleate cells of granulomas, as in tuberculosis.

What activates these macrophages?

Cytokines from T cells that recognise heart proteins, because those proteins look like streptococcal M protein.

Slide station
A section of lung from a 40-year-old woman with long-standing rheumatic mitral stenosis. The alveolar walls are thick and fibrosed, and the alveoli hold many macrophages full of brown pigment. Name the change.

Chronic passive congestion of the lung. The pigmented cells are heart failure cells: alveolar macrophages full of haemosiderin.

Where does the pigment come from?

Red cells leak from the congested capillaries into the alveoli. Macrophages engulf them and break the haemoglobin down to haemosiderin.

Why does mitral stenosis congest the lung?

The narrow valve raises the pressure in the left atrium, and that pressure passes back into the pulmonary veins and capillaries.

What happens to the right ventricle in time?

Pulmonary hypertension makes it hypertrophy, and then it fails.

Viva station
At the bench you're given a report for an 11-year-old boy with migratory arthritis and a new apical murmur. His throat swab culture grew nothing, and his ASO titre is above the laboratory's upper limit of normal. Tell me how you read it.

A raised ASO titre shows a recent group A streptococcal infection. Antibodies against streptolysin O stay raised after the organism has gone from the throat, so the titre gives the evidence the swab can't.

Why did the throat swab grow nothing?

Rheumatic fever starts two to three weeks after the sore throat, the time the immune response takes to build. By then the streptococci have been cleared, so throat cultures are usually negative.

Where does this result fit in the Jones criteria?

It's the evidence of a recent streptococcal infection that the Jones criteria need, alongside two major criteria, or one major and two minor.

Which other antibody shows the same thing?

Antibodies against streptococcal DNase, measured as an anti-DNase titre.

Does the raised titre tell you his heart is inflamed?

No. An ASO titre shows recent infection only. It says nothing about whether the heart is inflamed or how badly a valve is damaged.

Viva station
At the bench you're shown an ESR tube from a 12-year-old girl with acute rheumatic fever, read at one hour. The red cells have settled 80 mm, with a long column of clear plasma above them. Tell me how you read it.

A raised ESR: the red cells have fallen far through their plasma in one hour. It shows inflammation, and a raised ESR is one of the minor Jones criteria.

Why do the red cells fall faster in inflammation?

The liver makes more fibrinogen. Fibrinogen neutralises the red cells' negative charge, the cells stack into rouleaux, and the stacks sink faster. Immunoglobulins raise the ESR too.

What else, apart from inflammation, raises the ESR?

Severe anaemia, pregnancy and age. Polycythaemia lowers it.

Her joints settled a week ago, but the ESR is still high. Why?

The ESR changes slowly, because fibrinogen and immunoglobulins change slowly. CRP falls quickly as inflammation settles, so it follows the illness from day to day.

Does a raised ESR show she had a streptococcal infection?

No. A raised ESR shows inflammation, but not where it is or what caused it. The evidence of infection comes from antibodies such as anti-streptolysin O.

At the bench: a 30-year-old woman with rheumatic mitral stenosis and atrial fibrillation has had a steady INR of 2.5 on warfarin for a year. She started metronidazole for a vaginal discharge a week ago. Her INR today is 5.6. Tell me how you read it.

Her INR is well above the target of 2.0 to 3.0 for atrial fibrillation. Her vitamin K-dependent factors are lowered further than the target needs, and a high INR is a risk factor for bleeding.

Which clotting factors has warfarin lowered, and which of them does the prothrombin time miss?

Factors II, VII, IX and X. The prothrombin time tests factor VII and the common pathway (X, V, II and fibrinogen), so it misses factor IX, which belongs to the intrinsic pathway.

Which tube was the blood taken in, and why?

Sodium citrate. Citrate binds calcium so the blood doesn't clot in the tube, and adding calcium back in the test reverses it. EDTA can't be reversed by calcium, so an EDTA sample is unsuitable.

What does the laboratory add to her plasma to start the test?

Thromboplastin (tissue factor with phospholipid) and calcium. The time the plasma takes to clot is the prothrombin time.

Why report an INR, not just the prothrombin time?

Thromboplastin reagents differ in sensitivity. The INR divides the patient's prothrombin time by the laboratory's normal mean and corrects for the reagent, so one sample gives the same INR anywhere.

Tell me what you know about rheumatic heart disease.

Rheumatic heart disease is the cardiac form of acute rheumatic fever, an immune-mediated inflammatory disease that follows a group A streptococcal sore throat. The acute attack is a pancarditis, with Aschoff bodies in all three layers of the heart. Each attack heals by scarring, and the scarred mitral valve narrows into a fish-mouth stenosis.

Why does a sore throat damage the heart?

Antibodies and T cells made against streptococcal M protein also recognise proteins in heart muscle and valves, which is molecular mimicry. The attack starts two to three weeks after the sore throat, when the streptococci have gone, and the inflammation heals by scarring.

Why does each new attack make the valve worse?

After a first attack, each new streptococcal infection can set off rheumatic fever again. Carditis worsens with each recurrence, and each attack adds more scar, so the damage is cumulative.

Why does a young woman with mitral stenosis have a stroke?

The narrow valve dilates the left atrium, and the dilated atrium fibrillates. Dilatation and fibrillation cause stasis, a mural thrombus forms, and a piece of it embolises to a cerebral artery and causes an infarct.

Tell me about the vegetations you can find on a heart valve.

There are four kinds. Rheumatic verrucae are small and warty, in a row along the lines of closure. Infective vegetations are large, friable and destructive, and hold organisms. Nonbacterial thrombotic vegetations are bland and loosely attached, in hypercoagulable states, most often cancer. Libman-Sacks vegetations of lupus are small to medium and can sit on either surface of the leaflet.

Which of the sterile vegetations show inflammation in the valve beneath them?

Rheumatic and Libman-Sacks vegetations. Both sit on fibrinoid necrosis with inflammation and can heal by scarring. Nonbacterial thrombotic vegetations sit on a valve with almost no inflammation.

Which of them destroys the valve?

The infective vegetation. It can erode into the myocardium and form a ring abscess. The others don't destroy the valve, though rheumatic and Libman-Sacks lesions can scar it.

Why do nonbacterial thrombotic vegetations embolise so easily?

They're loosely attached, because there's no inflammation beneath them to anchor them. They break off and cause infarcts in the brain, heart and other organs.

Friable, bulky, destructive vegetations of fibrin, inflammatory cells and organisms on the heart valves, most often the aortic and mitral, and the tricuspid in people who inject drugs. They can erode into the myocardium as a ring abscess. Infected emboli cause septic infarcts and mycotic aneurysms where they lodge.

  1. How do you tell acute from subacute endocarditis?

    By tempo. Acute endocarditis is a rapid, destructive infection by a virulent organism, on a normal or deformed valve. Subacute endocarditis comes on insidiously over weeks to months, usually on a damaged or deformed valve, from an organism of low virulence.

  2. Which signs come from microemboli?

    Petechiae, splinter haemorrhages in the nail beds, Roth spots in the retina, painless Janeway lesions on the palms and soles, and painful Osler nodes on the fingertips.

Microbiology and Parasitology

Tell me what you know about group A streptococcus.

Group A streptococcus, Streptococcus pyogenes, is a Gram-positive coccus in chains. It's catalase negative and β-haemolytic on blood agar, and it carries the Lancefield group A carbohydrate. M protein is a major virulence factor.

  1. What does M protein do?

    It blocks complement activation and protects the organism from phagocytosis. Antibodies against it opsonise the organism, and the same response cross-reacts with heart proteins in rheumatic fever.

  2. Which toxin causes the haemolysis, and which antibody does it leave behind?

    Streptolysin O, an oxygen-labile toxin that lyses red and white cells. It's antigenic, so the body makes anti-streptolysin O, measured as the ASO titre.

  3. Which diseases follow a group A streptococcal infection after a delay?

    Rheumatic fever, two to three weeks after a sore throat, and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis after a throat or skin infection, caused by immune complexes.

Tell me how you'd show that a child with suspected rheumatic fever had a streptococcal infection.

The throat swab is usually negative by the time rheumatic fever starts, because the organism has gone. So I'd look for antibodies: a raised ASO titre, or raised antibodies against streptococcal DNase, shows a recent group A streptococcal infection.

  1. Why is the throat swab usually negative?

    Rheumatic fever is caused by the immune response, which takes two to three weeks to build. By then the streptococci have been cleared from the throat and are absent from the lesions.

  2. Does a raised ASO titre tell you the heart is involved?

    No. It shows only a recent streptococcal infection. It says nothing about whether the heart is inflamed or how damaged a valve is.

  3. What does a rapid antigen test detect, and what's its weakness?

    It detects group A carbohydrate antigen in a throat swab within minutes and is highly specific. It's only moderately sensitive in children, so a negative result is backed up by culture.

Tell me about infective endocarditis in a patient with rheumatic heart disease.

A scarred rheumatic valve is a common site of infection. Viridans streptococci from the mouth are the usual cause on a damaged valve and cause a subacute illness. Staphylococcus aureus causes acute, destructive endocarditis on normal or deformed valves.

  1. How do viridans streptococci reach the valve and stay there?

    Dental work releases them from the mouth into the blood. They make dextran, which lets them stick to a damaged valve.

  2. What is culture-negative endocarditis?

    Endocarditis in which no organism grows from the blood, usually because antibiotics were given before the cultures or the organism is hard to grow.

  3. What is the microbiological major Duke criterion?

    Typical organisms for endocarditis, such as viridans streptococci or Staphylococcus aureus, grown from two or more separate blood culture sets.

Haematology and Immunology

Tell me how a streptococcal sore throat damages the heart.

Antibodies and T cells made against streptococcal M protein cross-react with proteins in heart muscle and valves, which is molecular mimicry. Bound antibody activates complement and Fc receptor-bearing cells, and T cell cytokines activate macrophages. The response takes two to three weeks to build, and by then the streptococci are gone from the lesions.

  1. What type of hypersensitivity is acute rheumatic fever?

    Type II, antibody-mediated. Antibody binds an antigen fixed in the heart tissue and injures it through complement and Fc receptors.

  2. And post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis?

    Type III. Streptococcal antigen and antibody form immune complexes that deposit in the glomerular basement membrane and activate complement.

  3. What do the T cells do?

    T cells against the same heart proteins release cytokines, which activate macrophages, such as those in Aschoff bodies.

Tell me about the erythrocyte sedimentation rate.

The ESR measures how far red cells settle in their plasma in one hour. Fibrinogen neutralises the red cells' negative charge, so the cells stack into rouleaux and sink faster; immunoglobulins raise it too. The ESR is non-specific, changes slowly, and is raised by severe anaemia and by pregnancy. It's cheap and needs no electrical power.

  1. How does CRP differ from the ESR?

    CRP is an acute-phase protein made by the liver under IL-6. CRP rises within hours of tissue injury and falls quickly as inflammation settles, and anaemia, pregnancy and heart failure don't change it.

  2. Where do the ESR and CRP fit in acute rheumatic fever?

    A raised ESR or CRP is a minor Jones criterion. Either shows inflammation but not its cause, so neither can stand in for evidence of a streptococcal infection.

Tell me about Virchow's triad.

Virchow's triad names three causes of thrombosis: endothelial injury, stasis or turbulent blood flow, and hypercoagulability. In rheumatic mitral stenosis the abnormal-flow limb is at work. The dilated left atrium causes stasis, and atrial fibrillation adds turbulence.

  1. Which limb explains a left atrial thrombus in mitral stenosis?

    Abnormal flow: stasis in the dilated atrium and turbulence from atrial fibrillation.

  2. How does stasis lead to a thrombus?

    Stasis lets platelets and leucocytes touch the endothelium, slows the washout of activated clotting factors and keeps their inhibitors from flowing in.

Tell me about the INR.

The INR is the prothrombin time corrected for the thromboplastin reagent, so results agree between laboratories. The prothrombin time tests the extrinsic and common pathways. Warfarin lowers the vitamin K-dependent factors II, VII, IX and X, and the fall in VII, X and II raises the INR. The target on warfarin in atrial fibrillation is 2.0 to 3.0.

  1. Which tube do you take the blood in, and why?

    Sodium citrate. Citrate binds calcium, so the blood doesn't clot in the tube, and adding calcium reverses it in the test.

  2. The INR is in range two days after warfarin is started. Has warfarin's antithrombotic effect begun?

    Not yet. The prothrombin time rises early as factor VII falls, but the antithrombotic effect waits until factors X and II fall, which takes several days.

Pharmacology

Tell me about benzathine penicillin in rheumatic heart disease.

Benzathine penicillin is a slow-release form of penicillin G, given by intramuscular injection. One injection gives low but long-lasting levels. Repeated injections prevent new streptococcal infections, and so new attacks of rheumatic fever, which would add to the valve damage. In Nigeria it's continued for years, often for life.

  1. How does penicillin kill the streptococcus?

    Penicillin binds penicillin-binding proteins and blocks transpeptidation, the cross-linking of peptidoglycan. A growing bacterium can't build its wall, and it dies.

  2. What do you give a patient who's allergic to penicillin?

    Erythromycin. Erythromycin binds the 50S ribosomal subunit and stops protein synthesis. Its metabolites inhibit cytochrome P450, so it raises warfarin levels, and it raises digoxin levels too.

Tell me about aspirin in acute rheumatic fever.

Aspirin eases the arthritis and fever of acute rheumatic fever. Aspirin blocks cyclooxygenase, so fewer prostaglandins are made. Aspirin irritates the stomach and can cause gastric and duodenal ulcers. Like other NSAIDs it can cause tinnitus, and it isn't given to children with a viral infection because of Reye syndrome.

  1. Why does aspirin's effect on platelets last so long?

    Platelets can't make new cyclooxygenase, so the block lasts for the rest of each platelet's life. Other cells make new enzyme within hours.

Tell me why a patient with mitral stenosis and atrial fibrillation takes furosemide and digoxin.

Furosemide is a loop diuretic. Furosemide blocks NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb, so salt and water are lost and the congested lungs clear. Digoxin inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, and its vagal effect slows conduction through the atrioventricular node, so the ventricular rate falls in atrial fibrillation.

  1. What are the signs of digoxin toxicity?

    Anorexia, nausea and vomiting; visual disturbances, including altered colour perception; and arrhythmias, such as ventricular bigeminy and atrioventricular block.

  2. Why does furosemide make digoxin more toxic?

    Furosemide causes hypokalaemia. Potassium and digoxin compete for binding to Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, so with less potassium more digoxin binds and toxicity rises.

Tell me what you know about warfarin.

Warfarin is an oral anticoagulant. Warfarin blocks vitamin K epoxide reductase, so factors II, VII, IX and X, and proteins C and S, are made without their γ-carboxyl groups and don't work. Its effect is followed with the INR. Its main adverse effect is bleeding, and vitamin K reverses it.

  1. Why can warfarin cause skin necrosis early in treatment?

    Protein C has a short half-life, like factor VII, so it falls first and the blood clots more easily for a short time. In inherited protein C deficiency, skin necrosis can follow in the first weeks.

  2. Name drugs that raise the INR and drugs that lower it.

    Metronidazole, co-trimoxazole, fluconazole and erythromycin slow warfarin's breakdown and raise the INR. Rifampicin and barbiturates induce liver enzymes and lower it.

  3. Why isn't warfarin given in early pregnancy?

    Warfarin crosses the placenta. Early exposure causes fetal warfarin syndrome, with abnormal bone formation, and warfarin can cause bleeding in the fetus.

    Sudden intravascular haemolysis in falciparum malaria, with haemoglobin in the urine turning it dark, and acute kidney injury.
    Blood schizonticide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the asexual parasites inside red cells, the stage that causes the illness. Every drug that treats malaria is one.
    C-peptide
    A peptide the pancreas releases along with insulin. It's raised when the patient's own pancreas makes too much insulin, and suppressed when insulin is injected.
    Caseous necrosis (caseation)
    Necrosis in which dead tissue loses all structure and becomes soft, granular and cheese-like. Down the microscope it has no nuclei and no preserved architecture.
    Caspase
    One of a family of enzymes that carry out apoptosis.
    CD21
    A receptor on B cells for the complement fragment C3d. Epstein-Barr virus binds CD21 to get into the B cell.
    CD40
    A receptor on B cells. A helper T cell engages it through CD40 ligand to make the B cell divide. LMP1 of Epstein-Barr virus mimics CD40 and is switched on all the time.
    Cell cycle–nonspecific drug
    A cytotoxic drug that kills cells whether they're cycling or resting in G0, such as cyclophosphamide or doxorubicin. Cycling cells are still more sensitive to it.
    Cell cycle–specific drug
    A cytotoxic drug that kills cells only while they pass through one phase of the cell cycle: methotrexate and cytarabine in S phase, vincristine in M phase.
    Cell-mediated immunity
    Defence carried out by T cells. Sensitised T cells activate macrophages so that they can kill the organisms they have phagocytosed.
    Cinchonism
    Tinnitus, headache, nausea, dizziness, flushing and blurred vision caused by quinine at treatment doses. Quinine comes from the bark of the cinchona tree.
    Class switching
    A change in the heavy-chain constant region a B cell uses, for example from IgM to IgG. It cuts and rejoins the heavy-chain gene in the germinal centre.
    Coagulative necrosis
    Necrosis in which the dead cells keep their outline for some days, so the tissue's architecture can still be seen.
    Cofactor
    A cause that helps a tumour arise but can't produce it alone. Epstein-Barr virus is a cofactor in endemic Burkitt lymphoma.
    Commissural fusion
    Scarring that joins neighbouring valve leaflets or cusps at their edges, so the valve can't open fully. It's a sign of past inflammation.
    Counter-regulatory hormones
    Hormones that raise plasma glucose and oppose insulin: glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol and growth hormone.
    Culture-negative endocarditis
    Infective endocarditis in which no organism grows from blood cultures, usually because antibiotics were given first or the organism is hard to grow.
    Cyclooxygenase (COX)
    The enzyme that turns arachidonic acid into prostaglandins and, in platelets, thromboxane. Aspirin and other NSAIDs block it, which lowers inflammation, pain and fever.
    Cytochrome P450
    A family of liver enzymes that break down many drugs, warfarin among them. A drug that inhibits these enzymes raises the level of other drugs; a drug that induces them lowers it.
    Dactylitis (hand-foot syndrome)
    Painful swelling of the hands and feet caused by infarcts of their small bones. It's often the first presentation of sickle cell disease.
    Dihydrofolate reductase
    The enzyme that reduces dihydrofolate back to tetrahydrofolate, the active form of folate used to make DNA. Proguanil blocks the malaria parasite's own form of it.
    Duffy antigen (DARC)
    A chemokine receptor on the red cell surface. Plasmodium vivax uses it to enter the red cell, and most people of West African origin lack it.
    Dystrophic calcification
    Calcium deposited in dead or damaged tissue while the blood calcium is normal.
    EBER (EBV-encoded small RNA)
    Small RNA molecules made in large amounts by Epstein-Barr virus in a latently infected cell. A stain for EBER on a tissue section shows which cells carry the virus.
    Encapsulated bacteria
    Bacteria wrapped in a polysaccharide capsule that resists phagocytosis, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b and Neisseria meningitidis. Antibody against the capsule lets phagocytes take them up.
    Endoperoxide bridge
    The bridge of two linked oxygen atoms in the artemisinin molecule. Iron is thought to split it, and the free radicals released kill the malaria parasite.
    Epithelioid macrophage
    An activated macrophage with abundant cytoplasm and an elongated nucleus. These cells form the wall of the granuloma.
    Erythema marginatum
    A red, ring-shaped rash on the skin, one of the major Jones criteria for acute rheumatic fever.
    Euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis
    Diabetic ketoacidosis with a plasma glucose that's normal or only a little raised. SGLT2 inhibitors can cause it in patients who are very short of insulin.
    Extranodal
    Arising outside the lymph nodes, in an organ such as the jaw, bowel or kidney. Burkitt lymphoma usually arises at extranodal sites.
    Extravascular haemolysis
    Destruction of red cells by macrophages, rather than inside the blood vessels. It causes jaundice and pigment gallstones.
    Fat necrosis
    Death of fat cells, from released lipases or from injury, with chalky white deposits where fatty acids bind calcium.
    Fc receptor
    A receptor on macrophages, neutrophils and other leucocytes that binds the tail (Fc part) of an antibody, so the cell can engulf or attack what the antibody has coated.
    Ferritin
    The protein that stores iron. Its plasma level usually follows the iron stores, but it's an acute-phase protein, so inflammation raises it too.
    Fetal warfarin syndrome
    Birth defects, chiefly abnormal bone formation, after a fetus is exposed to warfarin early in pregnancy. Warfarin crosses the placenta and blocks the carboxylation of fetal bone proteins.
    Fibrinoid necrosis
    Necrosis of a vessel wall where immune complexes or leaked plasma proteins are deposited, staining bright pink.
    Fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH)
    A test that binds fluorescent DNA probes to chosen genes in a cell's nucleus. A probe pair can show a gene broken apart or moved to a new chromosome, even in cells that aren't dividing.
    Flow cytometry
    A test that reads fluorescent antibodies bound to cells in suspension, cell by cell. It works on blood, marrow aspirate or disaggregated lymph node tissue.
    Free radical
    A molecule with an unpaired electron, which reacts with and damages lipids, proteins and DNA.
    Fructosamine
    Glucose bound to plasma proteins, mostly albumin. It reflects glucose control over the last few weeks, and it's used when HbA1c can't be trusted.
    Functional asplenia (hyposplenism)
    A spleen that's present but can't do its work of filtering the blood and clearing bacteria. In sickle cell disease it starts in early childhood, while the spleen is still large.
    Gametocide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the sexual forms of the parasite, the gametocytes, so they can't pass the infection on to a mosquito.
    Gametocyte
    The male or female sexual form of Plasmodium in red cells. A biting mosquito takes gametocytes up, and the cycle goes on in the mosquito.
    Gangrene
    A clinical term for necrosis of a limb or part, usually from loss of blood supply.
    Germinal centre
    The pale centre of a lymphoid follicle, where activated B cells divide fast and switch and mutate their immunoglobulin genes. Burkitt lymphoma arises from a germinal centre B cell.
    Gluconeogenesis
    The making of new glucose, mainly in the liver, from sources such as lactate and amino acids. Insulin suppresses it, and metformin reduces it.
    GLUT4
    The glucose transporter of muscle and fat cells. Insulin moves it from inside the cell to the cell membrane, so these cells take up glucose.
    Glycation
    Binding of glucose to a protein without an enzyme. The higher the blood glucose, the faster it happens.
    Granuloma
    A compact collection of epithelioid macrophages, often with multinucleate giant cells, ringed by lymphocytes.
    Growth fraction
    The share of a tumour's cells that are actively passing through the cell cycle. In Burkitt lymphoma it's nearly all of them.
    HACEK group
    Haemophilus, Actinobacillus, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella and Kingella: commensals of the mouth that are less common causes of infective endocarditis.
    Haemoglobinopathy
    An inherited disorder caused by a mutation that changes the structure of a globin chain, such as sickle haemoglobin (Hb S) or Hb C.
    Haemosiderin
    A golden-brown pigment of stored iron, seen where red cells have broken down or iron is in excess.
    Haemozoin (malarial pigment)
    A brown-black pigment the parasite leaves from the haemoglobin it digests. Ruptured red cells release it, and macrophages in the spleen, liver and marrow take it up.
    Haptoglobin
    A plasma protein that binds free haemoglobin. The pair is removed from the circulation, so the haptoglobin level falls in haemolysis.
    Haemoglobin electrophoresis
    A test that moves haemoglobins along a strip in an electric field, so each kind shows as a band at its own position.
    Heart failure cell
    A macrophage in the alveoli laden with haemosiderin from red cells that leaked out of congested lung capillaries.
    Hepcidin
    A hormone that keeps iron inside macrophages and blocks its absorption from the gut. Inflammation raises it, which starves the marrow of iron.
    Heterophile antibody
    An antibody made in infectious mononucleosis that agglutinates sheep red cells. The Monospot test detects it.
    Holoendemic malaria
    Malaria transmission that runs almost all year, so children are bitten by infected mosquitoes again and again from infancy.
    Howell-Jolly body
    A small remnant of DNA inside a red cell. Howell-Jolly bodies show on the blood film when the spleen has stopped working.
    High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)
    A test that passes haemoglobin through a column. Each kind leaves at its own time, so the machine names it and measures its share of the total.
    HRP2 (histidine-rich protein 2)
    A protein made by P. falciparum and no other species, and the antigen most malaria rapid diagnostic tests detect.
    Hyaline arteriolosclerosis
    Thickening of an arteriole's wall by glassy, pink, structureless material leaked from the plasma, which narrows the lumen. Hypertension, age and diabetes mellitus cause it.
    Hyperplasia
    An increase in the number of cells in a tissue, in cells that can divide.
    Hypersplenism
    An enlarged spleen with a fall in at least one blood cell line while the bone marrow works normally.
    Hypertrophy
    An increase in the size of cells, and so of the organ, without more cells.
    Hypnozoite
    A dormant liver form of P. vivax and P. ovale. Hypnozoites can wake weeks or years later and cause a relapse.
    Hypoglycaemia unawareness
    Loss of the autonomic warning signs of a low glucose after frequent episodes of hypoglycaemia, so the patient doesn't act until confusion or coma sets in.
    Hypoxia
    Too little oxygen reaching the cells.
    Immunohistochemistry
    Staining a tissue section with labelled antibodies, so cells that carry a given protein turn brown. The tissue's architecture is kept.
    Immunophenotype
    The set of proteins a cell carries, shown with labelled antibodies. In a lymphoma it tells you which kind of lymphocyte the tumour copies, and at which stage.
    Impaired fasting glucose (IFG)
    A fasting venous plasma glucose from 6.1 up to 6.9 mmol/L, with a 2-hour OGTT glucose below 7.8. A stage between normal and diabetes mellitus.
    Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT)
    A 2-hour OGTT venous plasma glucose from 7.8 up to 11.1 mmol/L, with a fasting glucose below 7.0. Some people with it go on to develop diabetes mellitus.
    Incretin
    A gut hormone released after a meal that boosts insulin release from the β cells. GLP-1 and GIP are the two main incretins, and DPP-4 breaks them down.
    Infarct
    An area of ischaemic necrosis caused by loss of the blood supply, in sickle cell disease usually from vessels blocked by sickled cells.
    International normalised ratio (INR)
    The prothrombin time corrected for the thromboplastin reagent used, so a result means the same in any laboratory. A normal INR is about 1.
    Insulin resistance
    Failure of target tissues, mainly the liver, skeletal muscle and fat, to respond normally to insulin.
    Insulitis
    Infiltration of the pancreatic islets by lymphocytes, mainly T cells, while they destroy the β cells in type 1 diabetes.
    Intermittent preventive therapy
    Treatment doses of an antimalarial given at set times to people at high risk, such as pregnant women, whether or not they're infected.
    Intrathecal
    Injected into the CSF, so a drug reaches tumour cells in the central nervous system that drugs in the blood barely reach.
    Iron chelation
    Treatment with a drug that binds iron tightly so it can leave the body in urine or faeces. It treats the iron overload of repeated blood transfusion.
    Irreversibly sickled cell
    A red cell whose membrane has been damaged by many cycles of sickling, so it stays sickled even when it's carrying oxygen.
    Ischaemia
    Too little blood reaching a tissue: less oxygen, and less of everything else the blood brings and takes away.
    Karyolysis
    A nucleus fading away as its DNA is broken down.
    Karyorrhexis
    A pyknotic nucleus breaking into fragments.
    Karyotype
    A picture of all a cell's chromosomes, laid out in pairs. It needs dividing cells, so the cells are grown in culture first.
    ATP-sensitive potassium channel
    A potassium channel in the β cell membrane that closes when ATP rises. Closing it depolarises the cell and leads to insulin release. Sulfonylureas close it directly.
    Diabetic ketoacidosis
    Metabolic acidosis from ketone bodies, which the liver makes from fatty acids when there's too little insulin to stop fat breaking down.
    Ki-67
    A nuclear protein present in cells that are in the cell cycle. A Ki-67 stain gives the share of tumour cells dividing, called the proliferation index.
    Kussmaul breathing
    Deep, sighing breathing in metabolic acidosis. It blows off carbon dioxide, which lessens the fall in pH.
    Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)
    An enzyme that turns lactate into pyruvate and back. It's found in many tissues and leaks into the plasma from damaged cells, so a raised level shows cell damage but not where.
    Lactic acidosis
    A high anion gap metabolic acidosis from lactate building up in the blood. Its commonest cause is tissue short of oxygen, from poor perfusion, hypoxia or severe anaemia.
    Lancefield group
    A letter (A, B, D and others) given to a streptococcus by the C carbohydrate antigen in its cell wall.
    Langhans giant cell
    A multinucleate cell formed when macrophages fuse. Its nuclei sit in a horseshoe around the edge of the cell.
    Latency I
    The most restricted pattern of Epstein-Barr virus gene expression, with EBNA1 and the EBER RNAs and little else. Burkitt lymphoma cells show this pattern.
    Latent infection
    An infection in which the viral DNA stays inside the cell and makes few or no new virus particles. Epstein-Barr virus keeps its DNA in the nucleus as a circle.
    Light chain restriction
    Expression of only kappa or only lambda light chain by a population of B cells. Light chain restriction shows the cells are one clone: each B cell makes one light chain, but a normal, polyclonal population carries both, kappa more often than lambda.
    Lipofuscin
    A yellow-brown 'wear and tear' pigment of oxidised lipid, building up in long-lived cells with age.
    Lipohypertrophy
    A swelling of fatty tissue under the skin where insulin is injected into the same site again and again. Insulin is absorbed unevenly from it.
    Liquefactive necrosis
    Necrosis in which enzymes digest the dead tissue into a liquid.
    M protein
    A major virulence factor of group A streptococcus: a wall protein that blocks complement activation and protects the organism from phagocytosis.
    Malignant otitis externa
    Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection of the external ear canal that burrows into the mastoid bone, mostly in older people with diabetes mellitus.
    Merozoite
    The form of Plasmodium released when an infected liver cell or red cell bursts. Each merozoite invades a red cell.
    Metabolic syndrome
    A cluster of central obesity, high triglyceride, low HDL cholesterol, raised blood pressure and raised fasting glucose, linked by insulin resistance.
    Metaplasia
    The replacement of one mature cell type by another, better able to stand a particular stress.
    Metastatic calcification
    Calcium deposited in normal tissue because the blood calcium is high.
    Methaemoglobinaemia
    Haemoglobin in the blood with its iron oxidised from the Fe2+ to the Fe3+ state. Drugs such as primaquine can cause it, and the patient looks cyanosed.
    Microalbuminuria
    A urine albumin of 30 to 300 mg a day, too little for overt proteinuria. In diabetes mellitus it's one of the earliest signs of nephropathy.
    Diabetic microangiopathy
    Disease of small blood vessels in diabetes mellitus: capillary basement membranes become thick, yet the capillaries leak plasma proteins.
    Molecular mimicry
    An immune response to a microbe that also attacks the body's own tissue, because a microbial antigen looks like a self antigen.
    Mucormycosis (zygomycosis)
    Infection with moulds such as Rhizopus and Mucor, whose hyphae have no septa, branch at right angles and invade blood vessel walls.
    Multidrug-resistant (MDR)
    Resistant to antibiotics from several different classes at once, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus.
    Mural thrombus
    A thrombus attached to the wall of a heart chamber or of the aorta.
    Mycotic aneurysm
    An aneurysm caused by bacterial infection of an artery wall, where an infected embolus has lodged.
    Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (the sodium pump)
    The membrane pump that moves sodium out of a cell and potassium in, using ATP. Digoxin inhibits it in heart muscle, and potassium competes with digoxin for it.
    Necrosis
    Death of cells in a living tissue, with their membranes breaking and their contents leaking out, which brings in inflammation.
    Necrotising granuloma
    A granuloma whose centre has died. In tuberculosis the dead centre is caseous.
    Nephrosclerosis
    Diffuse scarring of the kidney from narrowed arteries and arterioles, which leaves a finely granular surface and a thin cortex.
    Neurogenic bladder
    A bladder that doesn't empty properly because the nerves that control it are damaged. In diabetes mellitus autonomic neuropathy causes it, and urine left standing lets bacteria multiply.
    Neuroglycopenia
    Brain dysfunction from too little glucose: faintness, dizziness and confusion, which can progress to coma.
    Nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesion)
    Ball-like nodules of laminated matrix at the edge of the glomerulus, almost pathognomonic of diabetes mellitus.
    Non-Hodgkin lymphoma
    Any malignant tumour of lymphocytes other than Hodgkin lymphoma. Most arise from B cells, and Burkitt lymphoma is one of them.
    Oncogene
    A gene that drives cell growth and division when it's mutated or overexpressed. MYC is the oncogene switched on in Burkitt lymphoma.
    Opsonisation
    Coating of a microbe with antibody or complement so phagocytes can recognise and ingest it.
    Osmotic diuresis
    A large flow of urine caused by a solute, such as glucose, that stays in the renal tubule and holds water with it.
    Osteomyelitis
    Infection of bone and its marrow. In sickle cell disease bacteria seed bone already damaged by infarction.
    P-selectin
    An adhesion molecule that helps sickle cells stick to the walls of blood vessels, one step in vaso-occlusion.
    Pancarditis
    Inflammation of all three layers of the heart: the pericardium, the myocardium and the endocardium, including the valves.
    Pancytopenia
    A fall in all three blood cell lines: red cells, white cells and platelets.
    Papillary necrosis (necrotising papillitis)
    Death of the renal papillae, the tips of the medullary pyramids, as a form of acute pyelonephritis. Diabetes mellitus, urinary obstruction and sickle cell anaemia are its main settings.
    Paraprotein
    One immunoglobulin made in excess by a single clone of B cells. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a narrow, dense band, usually in the gamma region.
    Parasitaemia
    Malaria parasites in the circulating blood, counted on a film as the share of red cells infected or the number per microlitre.
    Penicillin-binding protein (PBP)
    A bacterial enzyme that cross-links the peptide chains of peptidoglycan, which gives the cell wall its strength. Beta-lactam antibiotics bind its active site and block it.
    Pigment gallstone
    A gallstone made of calcium bilirubinate. It forms when bile carries a high load of unconjugated bilirubin, as in chronic haemolysis.
    pLDH (Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase)
    An enzyme of the malaria parasite, detected by some rapid diagnostic tests. pLDH tests turn negative within days of cure.
    Polyclonal
    Coming from many different B-cell clones at once, rather than from one. Malaria and early Epstein-Barr virus infection both drive polyclonal B-cell activation.
    Polyclonal response
    Immunoglobulins made by many B-cell clones at once, as in chronic infection. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a broad, diffuse rise in the gamma region.
    Polymerisation of Hb S
    Deoxygenated sickle haemoglobin molecules binding one another into long fibres. The fibres distort the red cell into a sickle shape.
    Polymicrobial infection
    Infection with more than one organism at the same time. Infection that spreads into tissue from an open wound is often polymicrobial.
    Polyol (sorbitol) pathway
    Conversion of glucose to sorbitol by aldose reductase. It uses up NADPH, so glutathione can't be regenerated and the cell is left open to oxidative stress.
    PPAR-γ
    Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma, a nuclear receptor in muscle, fat and liver that controls genes for glucose and fat handling. Pioglitazone activates it.
    Prodrug
    A drug given in an inactive form that the body converts into the active drug. Cyclophosphamide is one: liver cytochrome P450 enzymes activate it.
    Prothrombin time (PT)
    The time citrated plasma takes to clot after thromboplastin and calcium are added. It tests the extrinsic and common clotting pathways.
    Pseudohyperkalaemia
    A potassium result raised in the tube, not in the patient: potassium leaks out of cells after the blood is taken, as in a haemolysed sample or one left unseparated.
    Pseudohypoglycaemia
    A falsely low glucose result, because blood cells kept using glucose in a tube with no fluoride before the plasma was separated.
    Purine
    A nitrogen-containing base of DNA and RNA: adenine or guanine. In humans, purines that aren't reused are broken down to urate.
    Pyknosis
    A nucleus that has shrunk and stains dark, as its chromatin condenses.
    Pyuria
    Neutrophils in the urine, which can make it look turbid. It's a sign of urinary infection such as acute pyelonephritis.
    Radical cure
    Treatment that clears malaria parasites from both the blood and the liver, so P. vivax or P. ovale can't relapse. It needs a blood-stage drug plus primaquine or tafenoquine.
    Recrudescence
    The return of malaria from blood-stage parasites that treatment didn't clear. A relapse is different: it comes from dormant liver forms.
    Renal threshold for glucose
    The plasma glucose, about 10 mmol/L, above which the renal tubules can't reabsorb all the filtered glucose, so glucose appears in the urine.
    Ischaemia–reperfusion injury
    Further injury to ischaemic tissue when its blood supply comes back.
    Reticulocyte
    A young red cell, just out of the marrow, that still holds RNA. The reticulocyte count shows how well the marrow is making red cells.
    Reye syndrome
    An illness of children and adolescents linked to taking aspirin during a viral infection, with or without fever. Aspirin isn't given to children or adolescents with a viral illness.
    Ribonucleotide reductase
    The enzyme that turns ribonucleotides into the deoxyribonucleotides a cell needs to copy its DNA. Blocking it stops dividing cells in the S phase of the cell cycle.
    Ring abscess
    An abscess in the tissue around a valve ring, where an infective vegetation has eroded into the myocardium beneath it.
    Ring haemorrhage
    A small haemorrhage in the brain's white matter arranged as a ring around a small vessel, seen at autopsy in cerebral malaria.
    Rouleaux
    Red cells stacked like a pile of coins. Fibrinogen and immunoglobulins cause the stacking, and the stacks settle fast, raising the ESR.
    Sanctuary site
    A tissue that drugs in the blood reach poorly, such as the brain and the testes, so tumour cells there can survive treatment.
    Schizont
    A Plasmodium form whose nucleus has divided many times. It breaks up into merozoites when the liver cell or red cell bursts.
    Schüffner's dots
    Fine dots in a red cell infected with P. vivax or P. ovale, seen on a well-stained Giemsa film as the parasite matures.
    Senescence
    A permanent stop to cell division that cells reach with age.
    Sequestration
    Red cells carrying maturing P. falciparum sticking to the endothelium of small vessels, through the parasite protein PfEMP1, so they leave the circulating blood and block the capillaries.
    SGLT2 (sodium-glucose co-transporter 2)
    The transporter in the proximal tubule that reabsorbs most of the glucose filtered by the kidney. SGLT2 inhibitors block it, so glucose is lost in the urine.
    Siderofibrotic nodule
    A small nodule of fibrous tissue holding iron pigment and calcium, found in the scarred spleen of sickle cell disease beside vessels full of sickled cells.
    Hepatic sinusoidal lymphocytosis
    Lymphocytes filling the sinusoids of the liver on biopsy. It supports a diagnosis of tropical splenomegaly syndrome but isn't specific to it.
    Solubility product
    The highest product of two ion concentrations that a solution can hold. Above it, the salt comes out of solution, as calcium phosphate does when plasma phosphate is high.
    Somatic hypermutation
    Random mutation of the variable-region genes of immunoglobulin in dividing germinal centre B cells. Cells whose new antibody binds antigen better are selected to survive.
    Pitting
    The spleen's removal of inclusions, such as Howell-Jolly bodies and Heinz bodies, from red cells it then lets go back into the blood.
    Splenic sequestration
    Sudden pooling of sickled red cells in the spleen, which enlarges while the haemoglobin falls. Seen mostly in infants with sickle cell disease.
    Splenomegaly
    Enlargement of the spleen. A massive spleen can reach the right iliac fossa.
    Sporozoite
    The form of Plasmodium that an infected female Anopheles mosquito injects when it bites. Sporozoites travel in the blood to the liver.
    Starry-sky pattern
    Pale tingible body macrophages scattered through sheets of dark tumour cells, seen at low power. Typical of Burkitt lymphoma, but not specific to it.
    Steatosis (fatty change)
    Triglyceride building up inside cells, seen most in the liver.
    Streptolysin O
    An oxygen-labile toxin of group A streptococcus that lyses red and white cells. It's antigenic, so infection is followed by anti-streptolysin O antibodies.
    Sydenham chorea
    A neurological disorder of acute rheumatic fever with involuntary, purposeless, rapid movements.
    Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)
    A DNA polymerase made by precursor B and T cells, before they mature. A positive TdT stain marks a lymphoblast.
    Tingible body macrophage
    A benign macrophage that has eaten the nuclear debris of apoptotic cells. In Burkitt lymphoma these macrophages form the pale stars of the starry sky.
    Tissue schizonticide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the parasites developing or lying dormant in the liver, such as primaquine.
    Chromosomal translocation
    A break in two chromosomes with an exchange of the broken pieces. In Burkitt lymphoma, t(8;14) moves MYC on chromosome 8 next to the immunoglobulin heavy-chain gene on chromosome 14.
    Trophozoite
    The growing stage of Plasmodium inside a red cell. On a stained film the young trophozoite looks like a ring.
    Tumour lysis syndrome
    The chemical upset when many tumour cells die at once, usually as treatment starts: urate, potassium and phosphate rise, calcium falls, and the kidneys can fail.
    Tumour marker
    A substance in the blood that a tumour makes or releases. In some tumours its level relates to the tumour's mass or spread, and a fall or a new rise can follow treatment and recurrence.
    Type II hypersensitivity
    Tissue injury caused by IgG or IgM antibodies that bind antigens fixed on cells or in the tissue matrix.
    Type III hypersensitivity
    Tissue injury caused by antigen-antibody (immune) complexes that deposit in vessel walls or glomeruli and activate complement.
    Unconjugated bilirubin
    Bilirubin that the liver hasn't yet joined to glucuronate. It isn't water-soluble, so it travels in the plasma bound to albumin and doesn't pass into the urine.
    Urobilinogen
    A colourless product that gut bacteria make from bilirubin. Some is absorbed and sent back into bile by the liver, and a little passes in the urine.
    Vaso-occlusion
    Blockage of small blood vessels by sickled red cells, which causes ischaemia, pain and infarction in the tissue they supply.
    Vegetation
    A thrombus on a heart valve. It may be sterile, or hold organisms, as in infective endocarditis.
    Verrucae (rheumatic vegetations)
    Small, warty vegetations in a row along the lines of closure of a valve in acute rheumatic fever, made of fibrinoid necrosis and fibrin.
    Villous lymphocyte
    A lymphocyte with fine hair-like projections from its cytoplasm. Villous lymphocytes in the blood suggest splenic marginal zone lymphoma.
    Virchow's triad
    The three causes of thrombosis: endothelial injury, stasis or turbulent blood flow, and hypercoagulability of the blood.
    Viridans streptococci
    Alpha-haemolytic streptococci that are normal flora of the mouth. They're the usual cause of subacute infective endocarditis on a damaged valve.
    Vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR)
    The liver enzyme that turns used vitamin K epoxide back into active vitamin K, so clotting factors can go on being carboxylated. Warfarin blocks it. Its gene is VKORC1.
    Whipple's triad
    A low plasma glucose, neuroglycopenic symptoms, and relief of the symptoms when the glucose is raised. Together they show that symptoms come from hypoglycaemia.
    Xanthine oxidase
    The liver enzyme that oxidises hypoxanthine to xanthine, and xanthine to urate. Allopurinol inhibits it.
